How to brief an agency so you actually get what you want

The difference between a useful brief and a vague one usually decides how the whole project goes.

BE
Benjamin Emmanuel
Founder, Dynatrix Tech · 6 min read

The single biggest predictor of whether a project goes smoothly isn't the agency's skill — it's how clearly the client can articulate what they actually need. Here's what separates a brief that gets you a great result from one that leads to a lot of "that's not quite what I meant."

Start with the problem, not the solution

"I need a new homepage" is a solution. "People land on our site and leave without understanding what we actually do" is a problem. The second version gives us far more room to solve it well — sometimes the real fix isn't a new homepage at all, it's clearer copy or a different navigation structure. Leading with the problem lets expertise actually help you.

Show examples, but explain why you like them

"I like this site" without explanation is nearly useless — we don't know if you like the color palette, the layout, the tone of the copy, or something else entirely. "I like how this site's pricing page makes the decision feel simple" tells us exactly what to borrow and what to ignore.

"I need a new homepage" is a solution. "People land and leave without understanding what we do" is a problem.

Be honest about your real constraints upfront

Budget, timeline, and who actually has final approval — say these plainly at the start. We've seen projects go sideways not because of bad work, but because the real budget ceiling or the real decision-maker only surfaced three weeks in. None of this information embarrasses you; withholding it just costs everyone time.

One point of contact, one final say

Feedback from five stakeholders with five different opinions, none of them empowered to make a final call, is the single most common cause of a project stalling out. It doesn't need to be one person doing all the work — it just needs to be one person who can say "yes, ship it" when opinions conflict.

What we actually do with a brief once we have it

We turn it into a short, written scope of work before any design or build starts — confirming what we heard, in our own words, so any misunderstanding surfaces on day one instead of at final delivery. It takes an extra day upfront and saves weeks of rework later.

Key takeaways

  • Describe the problem you're trying to solve, not just the solution you've already assumed.
  • When sharing reference sites, explain specifically what you like about each one.
  • Designate one final decision-maker to avoid conflicting feedback stalling the project.
